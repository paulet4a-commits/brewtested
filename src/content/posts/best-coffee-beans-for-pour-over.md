## Why Pour Over Needs Different Beans Than French Press

This matters: the brewing method determines which beans will sing and which will disappoint.

French press brews by full immersion—beans steep in hot water for 4 minutes, extracting heavily. This environment suits darker roasts and oily beans because the immersion method can handle (and benefits from) the body and weight. Darker roasts make French press taste better.

Pour over brews by percolation—hot water flows through grounds for 2-4 minutes. This method highlights what immersion masks: origin character, brightness, complexity. Light and medium roasts reveal these qualities because percolation extracts more delicately. That clarity is the point. Darker roasts in pour over often taste flat or over-extracted.

Single-origin beans matter more in pour over because blend recipes are designed for espresso and milk-based drinks. In filter coffee, the origin becomes the hero. Ethiopian Yirgacheffe plays differently than Colombian Huila. This diversity is what makes pour over adventurous once you move past blends.

## How We Tested

Our testing protocol matched real home brewing, not lab conditions:

1. **Equipment**: Standard ceramic pour over dripper, Chemex, and a burr grinder set to medium-fine
2. **Water**: Filtered tap water heated to 200°F, consistent across all tests
3. **Brewing method**: 4-minute brew time, consistent pouring technique, 17:1 water-to-coffee ratio
4. **Evaluation**: Tasting for clarity (distinct origin notes), brightness (acidity character), and complexity (flavor development through the cup)
5. **Repetition**: 3 brews minimum per bean to account for technique variation

We brewed these beans in both standard pour over and Chemex to see which adapted best across equipment. All beans were whole bean, ground immediately before brewing. We tested both fresh bags (within 2 weeks of roast date where available) and standard retail stock.

## FAQ

**Q: Why is grind size so important for pour over beans?**
A: Pour over timing depends on even particle size. Too coarse, and water flows through without extracting enough flavor (sour taste). Too fine, and the brew becomes over-extracted (bitter). The right grind size for your beans and dripper setup is why a [good grinder for beginners](/posts/best-coffee-grinder-for-beginners/) is worth the investment. Blades grind unevenly; burrs produce consistency.

**Q: Should I buy whole bean or pre-ground?**
A: Whole bean always. Coffee oxidizes (loses flavor) rapidly once ground—pre-ground starts stale, especially in grocery stores. Grind fresh just before brewing. If you don't have a grinder yet, buy whole bean now and plan the grinder purchase for next. Your future self will taste the difference.

**Q: Can I use espresso beans in pour over?**
A: Yes, but not ideally. Espresso blends are typically darker and roasted for different extraction. They work in pour over (see Intelligentsia Black Cat), but they won't show the brightness or clarity that pour-over roasts deliver. It's like using a hammer to install a screw—it works, but there's a better tool.

**Q: How long do beans stay fresh?**
A: Peak flavor is roughly 1-2 weeks after roast. Usable for about 4 weeks. After that, they're not stale exactly, but the brightness fades and the cup tastes flatter. Check roast dates on bags. "Best by" dates are often misleading—roast date matters more.

**Q: What's the difference between light, medium, and dark roast?**
A: Light roast (blonde to cinnamon color) highlights origin flavors, bright acidity, lighter body. Medium roast balances acidity with caramel sweetness and more body. Dark roast emphasizes roasted flavors, heavier body, low acidity. Pour over traditionally pairs with light-to-medium because the clarity method doesn't need the body or heat that dark roasts bring.
